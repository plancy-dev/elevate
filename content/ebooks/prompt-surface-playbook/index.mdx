## How to use this playbook

This is a **compact operating guide**—not theory. Read it in order once, then keep it as a checklist when your team argues about “what good looks like” for prompts.

**You’ll get the most value if you:**

- Pick **one** customer-facing workflow (onboarding email, support macro, or campaign brief) as your first “surface.”
- Name **one owner** who can say “ship it” for that surface—not everyone in the channel.

---

## The thesis in one paragraph

When the work is language, **access to a model isn’t the bottleneck**. The bottleneck is **agreement**: what “good” means, who can change it, and how you know it didn’t drift last Tuesday in someone’s private tab. Treating the prompt as a **product surface** means you manage it with the same seriousness as a pricing page or a checkout flow—because for many customers, it *is* the product.

---

## A practical audit (30 minutes)

1. **Inventory** — List the top **five** prompts your org actually ships to customers or regulators (not demos). One line each: intent + owner team.
2. **Classify** — Tag each as **high risk** (money, compliance, brand) or **medium** (efficiency, internal). Only high risk needs heavyweight review *first*.
3. **Single source** — For one high-risk prompt, move the canonical text out of Slack into **one** place your team agrees on (doc, repo, or Elevate Productions episode notes).
4. **Diff habit** — When someone changes that prompt, capture **what changed and why** in one sentence. No essay—just enough for the next person to trust the edit.

---

## What “good” looks like (minimum bar)

- **Observable** — Someone who didn’t write the prompt can find it in **one hop** from the workflow it belongs to.
- **Scoped** — The prompt states **what it does not do** (no medical advice, no pricing guarantees) when stakes require it.
- **Tested** — At least **one** negative example the team agrees is out of bounds, written down next to the prompt.
